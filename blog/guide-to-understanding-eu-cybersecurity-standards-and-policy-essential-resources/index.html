A regulation, a standard and a certification scheme can appear in the same conversation. They answer different questions. It helps to know which document you are looking for before reading all of them.

Finding the right EU cybersecurity document

What does the law require? Which standard helps you implement it? Who writes that standard, and who checks conformity?

This guide brings together the EU laws, organizations and resources that explain those distinctions. It covers CRA, NIS2 and RED, along with bodies such as ENISA, ETSI and CENELEC. You can use it to find the relevant document without first memorizing every acronym.

What this guide covers

  • What’s the difference between a law, directive, and a standard?

  • How the New Legislative Framework (NLF) shapes cybersecurity policy
  • Who’s responsible for what — from ENISA to the TIC Council
  • Where to start if you want to contribute to EU policy or standards

Let’s Start With the Basics

What’s the difference between a Law, a Regulation, a Directive, and an Act?

First, separate the terms:

Term What It Means Is It Binding?
Law National rule passed by a government. Must be followed. Yes
Regulation EU-wide rule. Automatically becomes law in all member states. Yes
Directive EU sets the goal, countries choose how to implement it. Yes (indirectly)
Act A regulation or directive (used informally). Yes

The useful distinction is between what the law requires and how a standard explains implementing it.

Learn more about types of EU legislation here.

Understanding the New Legislative Framework (NLF)

How do the legal requirements connect to the work on a product or service? The EU uses the New Legislative Framework (NLF), which brings together:

  • Legislation (like directives and regulations) that define essential requirements
  • Harmonized standards that show how to meet those requirements
  • Conformity assessment procedures and market surveillance to check compliance

What’s a harmonized standard?
A harmonized standard is a European standard developed by a recognized European Standards Organization (e.g., CEN, CENELEC, ETSI) and published in the Official Journal of the EU. If a product complies with a harmonized standard, it benefits from presumption of conformity with relevant legal requirements.

What does presumption of conformity mean?
Following a harmonized standard means the product is presumed to meet the essential legal requirements, with no extra proof needed unless market surveillance says otherwise. Using the standard is optional; it offers a shorter route to demonstrating compliance.

Example: CRA and RED set cybersecurity obligations. CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI develop the standards to support them.

In the automotive world, cybersecurity is also governed by UNECE Regulations R155 and R156, which define requirements for vehicle cybersecurity management systems (CSMS) and software update management systems (SUMS) respectively. These are mandatory for new vehicle type approvals in the EU and many other markets.

What’s UNECE?
The United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) develops international legal instruments, including vehicle regulations that many countries — including all EU Member States — adopt. UNECE's World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations (WP.29) sets global standards on automotive safety, cybersecurity, and environmental impact.

Key EU Cybersecurity Law Deadlines (2025+)

Here are the dates and areas covered by the main laws on cybersecurity, data governance and digital resilience:

  • Cyber Resilience Act (CRA): Enters into force in 2025 with a 36-month transition period for most digital products. Establishes baseline cybersecurity requirements across hardware and software.
  • AI Act: Finalized in 2024 — introduces a risk-based framework for AI. High-risk systems must meet cybersecurity, transparency, and conformity requirements.
  • Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA): Applies from January 17, 2025. Covers financial institutions and critical ICT providers. Mandates ICT risk management, incident reporting, and resilience testing.
  • Data Act: Applies from September 2025. Sets rules for who can access and use data from connected products and services. Promotes data-sharing obligations, cloud interoperability, and limits vendor lock-in.
  • RED Delegated Act: Applies from August 2025. Wireless and IoT devices must meet cybersecurity requirements to be CE marked in the EU.

Who Regulates EU Cybersecurity? (Key Agencies & Bodies)

ENISA – The EU's Cybersecurity Agency

https://www.enisa.europa.eu
ENISA supports EU policy and publishes threat intelligence. It also runs the EU Cybersecurity Certification Framework and has a central role in the CRA and the Cybersecurity Act. Its publications are a useful place to start when you need context for these topics.

CEN & CENELEC – European Standardization Bodies

https://www.cencenelec.eu
Looking for the standards being written to support CRA or RED requirements? CEN and CENELEC draft European standards, with these areas of work:

  • CEN: General standards (AI, governance, risk)
  • CENELEC: Electrotechnical standards (IoT, smart devices, infrastructure)

ETSI – European Telecommunications Standards Institute

https://www.etsi.org
ETSI develops standards for cybersecurity, 5G, IoT and telecommunications. For IoT cybersecurity in Europe, its EN 303 645 is the reference to look at.

EFTA – European Free Trade Association

https://www.efta.int
EFTA includes Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland. These countries are not EU members, but adopt many EU laws and standards via the European Economic Area (EEA) or bilateral agreements.

  • EFTA countries participate in CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI
  • EU cybersecurity laws like NIS2 and RED are transposed into EFTA national law

Selling into an EFTA country? EU compliance is likely to matter there too.

TIC Council – Testing, Inspection & Certification

https://www.tic-council.org
Who does the testing and certification? The TIC Council represents the global third-party conformity assessment sector. Its labs and certifiers:

  • Evaluate compliance under schemes like EUCC, ISO 27001, Common Criteria
  • Support CE marking, RED compliance, CRA self-assessments
  • Are key players in conformity assessment procedures under the NLF

What’s a Notified Body?
A Notified Body is an organization designated by an EU country to assess the conformity of certain products before they are placed on the market. Notified bodies conduct audits, product tests, or certification for higher-risk products under CE marking regulations (including cybersecurity aspects under CRA and RED). Their involvement is mandatory when self-assessment isn’t allowed.

This is where the requirements become work to assess and verify: tests, inspections and certification.

ESAs – Supervisors of Digital Resilience in Finance

  • EBA (European Banking Authority)
  • EIOPA (European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority)
  • ESMA (European Securities and Markets Authority)

EBA, EIOPA and ESMA are the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs). Their responsibilities include implementing and enforcing DORA across the EU financial sector.

  • DORA applies to banks, insurance firms, investment platforms, and also to critical ICT service providers (like cloud hosting, security monitoring, and analytics).
  • It sets mandatory requirements for ICT risk management, incident reporting, operational resilience testing, and vendor oversight.
  • Enforcement starts from January 17, 2025.

Who’s not covered by DORA?
DORA targets financial entities and critical ICT providers. If you're building general apps or SaaS tools outside of this space, DORA probably doesn’t apply — though resilience principles still do.

ECSO – European Cyber Security Organisation

https://ecs-org.eu

ECSO brings together industry, researchers and institutions through a public-private partnership. Its work covers EU cybersecurity policy, innovation funding, SME growth and skills development.

EUR-Lex – The EU’s Legal Library

https://eur-lex.europa.eu
Need the legal text itself? EUR-Lex publishes the official, legally binding EU regulations, directives, delegated acts and decisions.

EU Cybersecurity Laws and Policies (Quick Reference)

Legal / Policy Text Why It Matters
Cybersecurity Act (EU 2019/881) Gave ENISA a permanent mandate and created the EU Cybersecurity Certification Framework (EUCC, EUCS, EU5G).
NIS2 Directive Applies to essential/important entities. Stronger rules, reporting, penalties.
Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) Sets baseline cybersecurity requirements for all digital products and software in the EU.
AI Act Risk-based framework for AI. High-risk systems must meet cybersecurity and conformity requirements.
Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) Applies to financial institutions and their ICT providers. Ensures they can withstand and recover from ICT-related incidents.
Data Act Sets rules on who can use and access data from connected products and services. Promotes interoperability and data-sharing obligations across sectors.
RED Delegated Act Wireless and IoT devices must meet cybersecurity criteria to be CE marked.
EU Cybersecurity Strategy The EU’s long-term plan for digital resilience and strategic autonomy.
UN Regulation R155 Mandates cybersecurity risk management across the vehicle lifecycle (CSMS) for vehicle manufacturers.
UN Regulation R156 Requires secure and controlled software updates, including over-the-air (OTA), through a Software Update Management System (SUMS).

Automotive-Specific Cybersecurity Rules

For mobility and automotive work, the relevant regulations include:

  • UN Regulation R155: Requires automakers to have a Cybersecurity Management System (CSMS) in place — covering the entire vehicle lifecycle. Mandatory for new type approvals from July 2022, and all vehicles sold after July 2024 in the EU.
  • UN Regulation R156: Focuses on Software Update Management Systems (SUMS), ensuring secure, traceable, and verifiable software updates — including over-the-air (OTA).

These are enforced via UNECE and applied under EU type approval law.

EUCC Cybersecurity Certification: What You Need to Know

EUCC stands for EU Common Criteria Scheme. It covers ICT products and is the first EU-wide cybersecurity certification scheme under the Cybersecurity Act. In practice, it:

  • Is based on ISO/IEC 15408 and 18045 (Common Criteria)
  • Covers products like firewalls, smart cards, secure elements, HSMs
  • Will eventually replace national schemes under SOG-IS

Certification is voluntary for now — but often required in defense, gov, and critical sectors.

What’s Next in EU Cybersecurity Standards (2025 and Beyond)

Beyond the requirements above, there are a few areas to follow:

  • Post-Quantum Cryptography standards (e.g., NIST selections + EU rollout)
  • Secure-by-design AI systems under the AI Act
  • Supply chain security and SBOMs becoming industry default

Which of these affects the systems you build? That is a more useful starting point than trying to follow every development at once.

Contributing as an engineer

This work is relevant to people who build products, not only to regulators. If you care about privacy, safety or product security, the links above give you places to start reading and contributing.

Start with the question you need to answer. Find the legal text, then the standards and assessment information that go with it. The names become easier to follow when you know why you need them.

Glossary: EU Cybersecurity Terms You Should Know

Term What It Means
CE Marking A declaration that a product meets EU safety, health, and environmental requirements.
Conformity Assessment The process to prove products meet legal requirements (testing, inspection, etc.).
Notified Body Independent orgs designated to assess higher-risk product compliance under EU law.
Presumption of Conformity If you meet a harmonized standard, you’re presumed to meet legal obligations.
UNECE UN body that sets international vehicle regulations, including cybersecurity (R155/R156).
CSMS Cybersecurity Management System – required by UNECE R155 for vehicle development and support.
SUMS Software Update Management System – required by UNECE R156 to manage secure vehicle software updates.
SOG-IS Older European framework for IT security certification, being replaced by EUCC.
DORA Digital Operational Resilience Act – EU regulation ensuring financial institutions and their tech providers can withstand ICT disruptions.
ESAs European Supervisory Authorities (EBA, EIOPA, ESMA) – enforce DORA in the financial sector.
ECSO European Cyber Security Organisation – a public-private partnership that supports EU cyber policy, innovation, and SMEs.
CRA Cyber Resilience Act – requires baseline cybersecurity for digital products in the EU.
RED Radio Equipment Directive – mandates cybersecurity for wireless/IoT devices.
